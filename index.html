§1

Abstract

Abstract

Seasonal reservoir storage in the western United States reflects two competing influences: climatic supply — winter snowpack and spring temperature governing runoff — and anthropogenic control — the managed release of water from the dam. We test which of these governs seasonal (May–June) change in storage at three Colorado reservoirs (Blue Mesa, Navajo, Pueblo) using ordinary-least-squares regression over 1980–2025, with a train/test design and multicollinearity screening on every model.

Before modeling, we established the credibility of the storage record with an independent, satellite-derived surface-area series (MNDWI + Otsu thresholding of Landsat 5/7/8/9, 1986–2025). Validated against the Bureau of Reclamation gauge record, the satellite product tracks the reservoir faithfully (R² = 0.986) but carries a persistent −7.5% areal underestimate that a sensor-era analysis attributes to spatial resolution rather than sensor transitions. Because that structural bias would compound when area is converted to volume, the driver models adopt the directly gauged storage in acre-feet as the response variable, retaining the satellite series only as an independent trend corroborator.

The controlled regression finds that managed release is the dominant, highly significant predictor of seasonal storage change at Blue Mesa and Navajo (release p < 0.001; test R² 0.89–0.94), while climate variables are significant predictors of river discharge — an upstream input — but not of storage change directly. We therefore fail to reject the null hypothesis that climate variables are not the primary direct drivers of seasonal storage change; climate's influence is real but mediated through inflow.

Research Question & Hypotheses

Primary question

Which hydrologic variables — climatic or anthropogenic — govern seasonal change in reservoir storage across Colorado's Upper Colorado and Arkansas basins?

The question is operationalized as a directional test on the May–June change in storage. Climate enters through winter snow-water equivalent (SWE) and spring temperature; the human factor enters through total managed release, with natural inflow held constant as a control.

H₁ · Alternative

Climate variables drive reservoir storage. Seasonal snowpack (SWE) and spring temperature are the primary drivers of seasonal reservoir storage change.

H₀ · Null

Climate variables do not drive reservoir storage. Seasonal snowpack and spring temperature are not the primary drivers of seasonal reservoir storage change; any climatic influence is not the governing signal at the seasonal timescale.

Test standard. A predictor "matters" only when it is statistically significant at p < 0.05 in the controlled model and holds up out-of-sample on a held-out test period — not merely when the model fits the training years.

Preliminary Analysis — Can the Storage Record Be Trusted?

Before asking what drives storage, we asked whether the quantity being modeled is measured well. An independent remote-sensing series provides that check: Landsat 5/7/8/9 Collection-2 surface reflectance was composited over the June–July window for each year 1986–2025, water was delineated with the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) under an automatic Otsu threshold (with a near-infrared guard to reject snow and bright edges), and the water pixels were summed to a surface area in km². The method needs no training data and applies identically across four decades — the invariance that trend detection requires.

Validation against the gauge record

The satellite area series was compared, year for year, against the Bureau of Reclamation gauged surface area (RISE) over the same June–July window (n = 40). The relationship is exceptionally tight in shape but offset in level.

Where the bias comes from

An underestimate that appears in every year is systematic, not random — so its source matters. Two candidates were tested. If the bias were a sensor artifact, it would shift when Landsat instruments changed (TM → ETM+ → OLI). It does not: residuals grouped by sensor era are essentially flat (−2.59, −2.32, −2.40 km²). The bias is instead resolution-driven — at 30 m, shoreline "mixels" that are part-water, part-land are dropped by the hard classification, trimming a thin margin of area along a long, complex shoreline. A spot-check against the area-capacity rating curve confirms this: the estimate matches the gauge closely at high fill but diverges at low elevations, where the exposed shoreline is most intricate.

The bias is correctable, but only empirically. A single linear calibration (truth = 1.104·est − 0.753) collapses the error from 7.5% to 1.1% (leave-one-out RMSE 0.40 km²). But that correction is fit to the very gauge record the model would otherwise use directly — so calibration does not create independent information about storage; it borrows it from the gauge.

Why the response variable is acre-feet

This is the decision the preliminary analysis exists to justify. The satellite path measures surface area, not storage. Turning area into a storage volume requires passing it through the area-capacity curve — which would fold the −7.5% areal bias into the volumetric estimate and compound it. The gauge, by contrast, reports storage directly in acre-feet (RISE item 76): a rated quantity with no dependence on image resolution and no classification step to bias.

Methods — Isolating the Human Signal

The core design is a controlled ordinary-least-squares regression of seasonal storage change on the human release term, with natural inflow entered as a control so the release coefficient carries only the human-caused portion of the signal.

Δstorage_af = β₀ + β₁·release_total_af + β₂·inflow_af + ε
ComponentVariableRole
Response (Y)delta_storage_afMay–June change in storage — the quantity being explained
Human (X)release_total_afWater released from the dam
Control (X)inflow_afNatural river inflow, held constant to isolate release

Estimation & validation. Models were fit by OLS with a 1980–2014 training period and a 2015–2025 held-out test period. A Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) check was run on every model to flag collinearity between release and inflow. Pueblo is the exception: a streamflow gap after 2007 forces a chronological fallback split (train 1985–2001, test 2002–2007), a drought-era window not directly comparable to the other two sites.

A parallel model (§7) tests the climate side by predicting river discharge from snowpack and temperature — quantities observable before spring runoff, and therefore usable as a genuine forecast, unlike storage which depends on future operational decisions.

Results — The Climate Signal (Snowpack + Temperature → Discharge)

The climate test targets discharge, not storage, and for a principled reason: snowpack and temperature can be measured before spring runoff begins, so they can genuinely forecast the water entering the system. Storage cannot be forecast this way — it depends on release decisions not yet made. Of two candidate snow measures, the full-season SWE (Nov–Apr mean) was selected over an April single-day snapshot for stronger out-of-sample reliability; the two are never combined, as they describe the same snowpack.

§8

Hypothesis Test — Outcome

The two result sets answer the question directly. The direct and dominant predictor of seasonal storage change is managed release (Task 1): highly significant at two of three reservoirs, and stable out-of-sample. Climate variables were significant for discharge — an input to the system — but were never the direct predictor of storage change, and even for discharge held at only two of three sites.

H₀ · ACCEPTED We fail to reject the null hypothesis

Within the seasonal (May–June) window, climate variables are not the primary direct drivers of reservoir storage change. Storage change is governed chiefly by anthropogenic release; climate's influence is real but enters one step upstream, through the inflow that release then regulates. On the storage-change question, the evidence supports H₀ over H₁.

Statistical note. Failing to reject H₀ is not proof that climate has no effect. It states that, at the seasonal storage-change timescale tested here, the managed-release signal dominates and any direct climatic signal is not distinguishable as the governing one. Climate remains a demonstrated driver of discharge (§7), and thus an indirect driver of storage over longer horizons.
§9

Discussion & Limitations

A two-tier hydrology. The results describe a system with a clear order of operations: snowpack and temperature set how much water arrives (discharge), and dam operations decide how much of it is held or released (storage). At the seasonal scale, the operational decision is the proximate lever on storage — which is why release dominates the storage model while climate dominates the discharge model.

Consistency of the strong signal. Navajo is the clearest case in both tasks, and Blue Mesa closely tracks it. That two independent basins produce the same structure is stronger evidence than either alone.

Pueblo, twice over. Pueblo resists both models, but for different reasons — a post-2007 data gap in Task 1 versus a genuine explanatory gap in Task 2. The Task 2 result is the more interesting: with full data, snowpack still fails to explain Pueblo's discharge, pointing to trans-basin diversions or a different dominant driver as a real hydrologic feature of the Arkansas system, not an artifact.

Measurement limitation. The satellite validation (§4) carries a structural −7.5% areal bias at 30 m resolution; this is why it informs trend rather than serving as the modeled quantity. The gauged acre-foot record is treated as ground truth, and RISE field schemas have historically shifted, requiring periodic parser maintenance.

§10

Conclusion & Next Steps

Across three Colorado reservoirs, seasonal storage change is driven primarily by managed release, not directly by climate — so the null hypothesis stands for the storage question, even as climate is confirmed to drive the discharge that ultimately feeds the reservoirs. A four-decade satellite record independently confirms the reservoirs are contracting and the gauge record is sound, while itself under-measuring area by a structural margin that justified modeling gauged acre-feet instead.

Carried into the final analysis

Two threads remain open. First, resolve the Pueblo storage gap — locate an alternate source to extend the usable Task 1 window past 2007, so Pueblo can be compared on the same footing as the other reservoirs. Second, investigate Pueblo's discharge — with SWE and temperature explaining almost none of its variation (test R² = 0.04), examine trans-basin diversions, lower-elevation snowpack, and other drivers upstream of the gauge.

Methods & software

Water delineation: MNDWI (green–SWIR1 normalized difference) with automatic Otsu thresholding and an NIR < 0.20 snow/bright-surface guard, on June–July median composites (30 m, EPSG:32613). Trend detection: Mann-Kendall with Hamed-Rao autocorrelation correction and Sen's slope (pymannkendall). Driver models: ordinary least squares with train/test validation and VIF screening (statsmodels, scikit-learn); analysis in pandas / numpy, figures in matplotlib.
